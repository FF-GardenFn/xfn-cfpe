# The Framework

The conceptual spine of `CAI/`, in five short sections. Each links to the document where the full treatment — and the data — lives. For results, start at [README.md](./README.md); read this page for the ideas that organize them.

The lineage in four sentences. **The idea:** treat alignment as a constraint on the space where task-optimization is allowed to occur, not as another objective inside it. **The insight:** enforcement deserves the name only when it is external to the thing it governs — a constraint you can argue with is an objective. **The bold move:** put the true objective outside the model's reward entirely, and read the model's disposition off choices it makes while optimizing something else. **The behavioral result so far:** an external gate sealed the action channel completely at zero benign-task cost, while self-critique alone could not — and the honest half of the story is that measuring *why* turned out to be the harder science.

## 1. A constraint you can argue with is an objective

Anything expressed in the channels a model optimizes over — constitutions in context, self-critique, refusal training — is an objective, and what a model can optimize *toward* it can learn to optimize *around*. A constraint, properly so called, binds regardless of the model's beliefs about it: [README.md](./README.md#design-principle-enforcement-independent-of-model-beliefs) states the defining property as *the model may know exactly what the kernel enforces and still be unable to violate it.* This is not a rhetorical distinction but a testable one: the K-disclosure axis ([k_disclosure_spec.md](./k_disclosure_spec.md)) turns it into an experiment by disclosing the gate in graded steps, K0–K3. Safety that persists under full disclosure is a constraint; safety that degrades was an objective wearing a constraint's costume.

## 2. Enforcement must be external to the governed entity

Constitutions work in governance and in code because the enforcer is not the enforced: courts interpret law, runtimes enforce protocols — an untrusted object does not get to implement its own reference monitor ([README.md](./README.md)). Two results close this argument from both directions. At inference time, self-critique fails open: in v4, constitution-as-text alone left residual violations on both models where the kernel's action channel was sealed ([results.md](./results.md)). At training time, negation neglect (arXiv 2605.13829) shows that normative wrappers around demonstrations are shed during internalization while the demonstrated behavior persists. Neither the channel the model reads nor the channel it is trained on can carry enforcement; only the runtime can.

## 3. Put the reward outside the reward

The K-pilot exposed the measurement problem the enforcement thesis creates: you cannot measure a constraint by waiting for a well-behaved model to attack it — 192 trials produced exactly one exercise of the gate ([k_pilot_results.md](./k_pilot_results.md)). The coupled-objective design ([coupled_objective_design.md](./coupled_objective_design.md)) inverts this. A hidden spirit-score $H$ is computed outside the model and coupled to a visible objective $V$ the model is happy to pursue; at perceived reward-parity, route choice reads out the disposition to game ($b$, the intercept) cleanly separated from optimization competence ($\beta$, the slope). The model cannot sandbag an axis it cannot see — stated as a bound with an explicit validity region, not an assertion. It is the externality principle of §2 applied to measurement: the gate keeps *actions* outside the model's control; the spirit-score keeps *measurement* outside its optimization.

## 4. Safety without a capability tax

The empirical face of the claim: 100% benign-task success in every condition of v4, alongside 0 executed tool-channel violations across all 760 kernel-condition trials — 380 per model ([results.md](./results.md)). Where governance does cost utility, the cost is concentrated and visible: boundary tasks — tempting-but-legitimate requests — degrade under both CAI and the kernel, and three-quarters of everything the gate blocked in v4 was a boundary task, not an attack (23 of 31 blocks; [analysis/v4_transcript_notes.md](./analysis/v4_transcript_notes.md)). The recovery loop pays most of that friction down. The shape of the trade is the point: a well-placed constraint prices safety at the *boundary*, where judgment is genuinely contested, rather than as a flat tax on capability.

## 5. Attribution is the hard problem

Every defect this project has had to correct was the same defect: **crediting one mechanism with work another did.** The scorer credited violations to models that were refusing (mention vs. use); the overloaded residual metric would have charged the gate with harms it had prevented; the pilot's first write-up credited the constraint with the objective's refusals ([POSTMORTEM.md](./POSTMORTEM.md), [k_pilot_results.md](./k_pilot_results.md)). The generalization: in a system governed by several layers at once — training, context, runtime — the hard problem is not *whether* behavior looks aligned but *which layer did the work*, and whether the behavior survives that layer's removal. That question has its own research surface in this repo ([control-provenance-eval](../project/control-provenance-eval/)), and the cheapest existing evidence is already in the v4 transcripts: under a constitution, refusals cite principles; at baseline, the same tasks are refused without them — stated rationale tracks the prompt's framing, not necessarily the governing cause ([analysis/v4_transcript_notes.md](./analysis/v4_transcript_notes.md)).
